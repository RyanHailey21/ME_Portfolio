## Overview

This capstone project built a quadcopter for UT Arlington's 3D-Printed Aircraft Competition, rotary wing category: the rules allow only 8 seconds of powered flight and a 60 ft maximum altitude, and the winner is whoever stays airborne longest after that. Our approach was to sprint the vehicle to altitude, cut power, and let a purpose-designed autorotation rotor on top of the airframe spin up and carry the whole aircraft down slowly, like a maple seed. The body and rotor mounting hardware are PLA; the rotor blades themselves are ASA aero, printed as airfoils. We won first place in the rotary wing category.

## Rotor Optimization

Fall time is entirely a function of the autorotation rotor's geometry, so before building anything we framed it as a numerical optimization problem. Using **AeroSandbox** and **NeuralFoil** to evaluate 2D NACA airfoil sections, we solved a nonlinear program in **CasADi** with the **IPOPT** solver, simulating the coupled fall/autorotation dynamics and maximizing time-to-ground. The optimizer searched over rotor radius, root and tip chord, blade twist, collective pitch, and the NACA 4-digit profile itself, all simultaneously rather than tuning each parameter by hand.

<Image src={CadModel} alt="OnShape CAD render of the full drone assembly with autorotation rotor blades" style="max-width: 800px; width: 100%; margin: 2rem 0; border-radius: 12px; box-shadow: 0 4px 24px #0002;" />

Once the optimizer converged on a blade geometry, we modeled the rotor and its one-way-bearing hub mount in **OnShape** and built out the full airframe assembly around it.

## Manufacturing the Blades

The rotor blades were the hardest part to manufacture. ASA aero is a foaming filament, and getting a clean airfoil cross-section out of it meant slicing with internal ribs in vase mode rather than solid infill. The bigger constraint was that the print could never pause the tool tip mid-layer: ASA aero's foaming action backdrives filament out of the nozzle during any stationary dwell, which floods the layer with stringing and ruins the print. Getting a travel-move-free toolpath through the slicer took several failed prints before it held together.

## Airframe Iteration

The body, arms, and rotor mount were printed in PLA. The first build used 60 mm arms with 3-inch driven propellers, and an early test flight showed the airframe didn't have enough yaw control authority: the drone spun uncontrollably on the way up, driven by reaction torque from the large autorotation rotor sitting on its one-way bearing on top of the body. Extending the arms to 85 mm and moving up to 4-inch propellers gave the driven motors enough yaw moment arm to cancel that torque, and the spin-up problem disappeared.

## Avionics and Autonomy

Flight came from a **BetaFPV F4** flight controller (2-3S) running **Betaflight** for low-level stabilization, powered by a 450 mAh 3S battery driving four 3500KV motors. An **ESP32-S3** rode alongside it, connected over the **MSP** protocol to read barometer, IMU, and vario data directly from the flight controller.

<Image src={ElectronicsStack} alt="BetaFPV F4 flight controller stack with ESP32-S3 companion board wired in" style="max-width: 550px; width: 100%; margin: 2rem 0; border-radius: 12px; box-shadow: 0 4px 24px #0002;" />

The ESP32 exposed a **Bluetooth LE** interface for starting the mission and calibrating altitude controller gains from a phone, with no need to reflash firmware between test flights. Onboard, it fused the flight controller's barometer, IMU, and vario data through a **Kalman filter** to get a clean altitude and vertical-speed estimate, then closed the loop with a cascaded controller that took over from an open-loop sprint phase once the vehicle passed 50 ft, precisely regulating the climb to the 60 ft ceiling. In the final seconds before the 8-second power cutoff, the ESP32 commanded a hard yaw input in the correct autorotation direction to pre-spin the rotor, so it was already turning at cutoff instead of spinning up from rest.

**First place, Rotary Wing category**, UT Arlington's 3D-Printed Aircraft Competition.

**Key takeaways:**
- Framing the rotor design as a coupled aerodynamic/dynamic optimization problem (AeroSandbox + NeuralFoil + CasADi/IPOPT) found a better blade than hand-tuning would have, and did it before any material was cut
- Foaming filaments like ASA aero impose real constraints on toolpath planning, not just print settings — vase mode and travel-free paths were required, not optional
- Yaw control authority has to account for reaction torque from any large free-spinning mass on the airframe, not just the driven propellers
- Reading state off an existing flight controller over MSP let us add a custom altitude autonomy layer without touching Betaflight's proven low-level stabilization
